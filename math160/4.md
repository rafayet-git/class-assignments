If the row echelon form of the coefficient matrix yields a consistent system with a free variable, it is useful to also create 0's above the leading ones. Such matrices are said to be in reduced row echelon form.

A matrix is in reduced row echelon form if 

1. it is in row echelon form

2. Any leading | is the only nonzero entry in its column

Examples:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & 4\\
0 & 1 & 0 & 2\\
0 & 0 & 1 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 \\
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 0 & 1\\
0 & 0 & 1 & 3\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

<u>Definition</u>: The process of using elementary row operations to translate a matrix into reduced row echelon form is called **Gauss-Jordan reduction** 

Use gauss-jordan reduction to solve the linear system

$$
3x_1 -6x_2 +12x_4 = 2\\
6x_1 -12x_2 + x_3 + 26x_4 = 6\\
-6x_1 + 12x_2 - 12x_4 = -1
$$

This is an undetermined system: less equations than unknowns

It can be inconsistent, but if it is consistent it must be infintely many solutions

This is because since you have more columns than rows, some columns do not have have a leading row, which corresponds to a free variable yielding infinitely many solutions.

$$
\left[

Application (electrical networks): Kirchoff's laws

1. At every node or junction, the sum of the incoming currents equals the sum of the outgoing currents.

2. Around every closed loop, the algebraic sum of the voltage gain equals the algebraic sum of the voltage drops.

The voltage drops $E$ for each resistor is governed by Ohm's law: $E=iR$, where $i$ represents the current in amperes + the resistance $R$ in ohms.
